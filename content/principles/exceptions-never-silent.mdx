Every real system needs exceptions. Inventory runs out after a plan is approved. Budgets get exceeded for good reasons. People go unresponsive against a deadline that will not move.

A system that forbids these does not prevent them. It pushes them offline — into email, phone calls and spreadsheets — where nothing can see them, nothing can verify them, and the record of what actually happened lives in someone's memory.

So the question is not whether to permit exceptions. It is whether the system can hold them.

Each one gets four things: an authorized path, a mandatory written reason, a notification to everyone affected, and a record that survives the transaction.

The evidence for this is not any single decision. It is that the same shape appeared three times on one project, months apart, on problems with nothing in common — inventory contention, sponsor budget overage, and an unresponsive approver. Consistency across unrelated problems is what makes it a position rather than a preference.

## Where it strains

The principle says nothing about *where* the record lives, and that turned out to matter. On the travel operations platform, two of the three exceptions produce a record only in the audit log, leaving package state unable to distinguish an approved plan from a substituted or proxy-confirmed one. Not silent, but buried — which is the failure mode the principle should have caught and did not.
